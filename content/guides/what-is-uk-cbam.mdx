The UK Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism, usually shortened to CBAM, is a charge on the carbon emissions built into certain goods when they are imported into the United Kingdom. The government describes the aim in one sentence: CBAM will make sure that highly traded, carbon-intensive goods imported into the UK face a comparable carbon price to the one paid by manufacturers in the UK.

This guide explains the shape of the mechanism. It does not replace the official guidance, which is linked in the sources panel and should be checked before you rely on any date or figure.

## What is being charged

CBAM is charged on the emissions embodied in a CBAM good when the good is imported. The Finance Act 2026 sets the charge as the sectoral domestic price for the good multiplied by the tonnes of carbon dioxide equivalent embodied in it.

The government's policy summary gives the calculation in one line:

<Callout title="The basic calculation">

CBAM liability = (embodied emissions × CBAM rate) − carbon price relief

</Callout>

Each part of that line has its own guide or page on this site:

- **Embodied emissions** can be based on actual, verified emissions data from the producer, or on default values that the government publishes. See [supplier emissions data](/guides/supplier-emissions-data).
- **The CBAM rate** is a single rate for each sector, published at the start of each quarter. See [rate watch](/rate-watch).
- **Carbon price relief** reduces the charge where a qualifying carbon price has already been paid on the emissions. See [carbon price relief explained](/guides/carbon-price-relief-explained).

## Which goods are covered

CBAM applies to goods from five sectors: <Rule k="sectorList" />. Being in a sector is not enough. Only the specific goods listed in the legislation are CBAM goods, and they are identified by commodity code. That is why the commodity code on your customs entry matters more than a product description. The guide on [commodity codes and scope](/guides/commodity-codes-and-scope) goes through it.

## Who is affected

The charge falls on the importer. HMRC's guidance on who counts as the importer says that where customs duties are payable, you are the person named on the import declaration, even if someone else submitted it for you. Where no customs duties are payable, you are the person importing the goods, even if an agent, freight forwarder or customs broker handles the paperwork.

Not every importer has to register. There is a registration threshold, currently <Rule k="registrationThreshold" /> of CBAM goods, tested in two ways. The guide on [how the threshold works](/guides/how-the-registration-threshold-works) explains both tests, and the [threshold checker](/threshold-checker) lets you try your own figures.

Some imports are outside the charge altogether, such as goods imported for private use and goods of UK origin. The policy summary lists the exemptions, and the threshold guide covers how they interact with registration.

## The dates to know

The dates below come from the policy summary and are held in one place on this site, so they update everywhere at once.

- The charge starts on <Rule k="startDate" />.
- In the first calendar year, businesses that become liable have until <Rule k="registrationDeadline" /> to register. Ordinarily the deadline is 30 days from the day you become liable.
- The first accounting period is twelve months. Its return and payment are due on <Rule k="firstReturnDeadline" />.
- From 2028, accounting periods are quarterly, each with its own return deadline set out in the policy summary.

## What to keep

Records must be kept for <Rule k="recordRetention" />, counted from the later of the date the record was created and the end of the accounting period it relates to. HMRC's record-keeping guidance lists the commodity code, tax point date, value and weight of each import, evidence of any exemption, emissions verification reports where actual data is used, and any carbon price relief documents.
