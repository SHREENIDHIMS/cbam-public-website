The amount of CBAM you pay depends on the emissions embodied in the goods. For every in-scope import, someone has to supply a figure for those emissions. The official guidance gives you two routes, and the route you can use for each line decides how much work lands on your suppliers.

## The two routes

The government's policy summary describes the options for emissions data as:

- **Actual verified emissions intensity data**; or
- **Government-published default emissions values**, which will be published in a notice on GOV.UK in advance of CBAM coming into force.

The Emissions and Verification Regulations 2026 set out how embodied emissions are calculated. Their structure mirrors the two routes: one part covers default values, one covers actual emissions, and a further part covers monitoring and verification of emissions data, including what is required to be a verifier.

This site does not hold the government's default values. Where a result on this site uses a default intensity, it is a labelled scenario value invented for illustration. Never use one in a return.

</Callout>

## Why supplier data matters

Default values are designed to be used when better data is not available. For many importers the most useful thing they can do early is find out which suppliers can provide actual data, and for which products. A supplier that can share verified figures may change the emissions basis for all of that supplier's lines. A supplier that cannot leaves you on defaults.

That is why the [liability preview](/liability-preview) ranks suppliers by how much of the indicative position they drive, and shows which ones still have missing data. ## What to ask a producer for

The regulations and HMRC's guidance are the authority on what the data must contain. As a practical starting list for a conversation with a supplier, ask for:

1. the production site and installation that made your goods, so the data can be tied to the right place;
2. the product or commodity code the figures relate to, matched to your own ledger;
3. the monitoring period the figures cover;
4. the embodied emissions per tonne of product, and the method used to calculate them;
5. whether the data has been verified, who verified it and the date, with the verification report or summary;
6. whether any of the emissions were subject to a carbon price, which is relevant to [carbon price relief](/guides/carbon-price-relief-explained).

Ask for the same fields from every supplier, in a consistent format. It makes the later checking much easier.

## What to keep

HMRC's record-keeping guidance says that if you report actual emissions, you must keep the verification reports or summaries that confirm the data. Together with your customs and import records, they need to be kept for <Rule k="recordRetention" />, counted from the later of the date the record was created and the end of the accounting period.

Treat supplier declarations the way you treat any evidence. A declaration marked unverified, or a figure with no method or period attached, is not the same thing as verified data. Record it, flag it and say what is missing.

## What can go wrong

- **Data for the wrong product.** A supplier makes several products at one site. Check that the figure matches the commodity code on your line.
- **Data for the wrong period.** Emissions figures cover a monitoring period. Check it against the period that applies to your imports.
- **Mixed units.** Emissions per tonne, per kilogram and per unit are all in circulation. Convert carefully and keep the original.
- **A declaration without evidence.** Keep it on file, but do not treat it as verified.
